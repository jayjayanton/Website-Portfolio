Connecting watershed data with environmental-flow research

I automated watershed delineation, modeled stream reference status and investigated hydrologic-alteration relationships across Texas ecoregions. The work combines R-based API integration with Python modeling and contributed to a coauthored River Research and Applications paper, while keeping exploratory model-fit curves distinct from validated ecological conclusions.

The problem

What needed to be understood or built?

Environmental-flow research needs to separate natural watershed context from human alteration and understand how hydrologic patterns vary across regions. That requires consistent watershed boundaries, reference-stream information and analysis that distinguishes model behavior from ecological evidence.

My contribution

The work I was responsible for.

I automated watershed delineation from stream-gage coordinates, developed a Random Forest workflow for reference versus non-reference streams, and explored relationships between hydrologic-alteration metrics and fish response variables across Texas ecoregions. I am a coauthor of the related 2026 paper on reconstructing historical hydrologic alteration.

Approach

How I worked through the problem.

01

Retrieve watershed geometry programmatically

Used R with the Model My Watershed API to submit gage coordinates, check delineation results and save watershed boundaries for spatial analysis.

02

Model reference-stream status

Used watershed and stream attributes in a Random Forest workflow. The original notebook used a regressor with rounded binary outputs; the later reusable script uses a classifier with train/test separation and missing-value handling in its modeling pipeline.

03

Explore hydrologic metrics and fish responses

Fitted multi-output decision-tree regressions for individual hydrologic-alteration metrics and multiple fish response variables. Repeated the models across tree depths to examine the change in training fit.

04

Compare patterns across ecoregions

Averaged the available metric-level R² scores at each depth within each ecoregion. Numerical derivatives identified candidate inflections in the mean curves for comparison, rather than declaring biological thresholds.

Result & scope

What the work delivered.

The work produced reusable delineation and modeling scripts, original ecoregion comparison figures and a contribution to a collaborative peer-reviewed publication. The exploratory curves make differences in fitting behavior visible; their purpose and limitations remain separate from the published team-wide hydrologic modeling results.

Scope & next steps

  • The displayed tree-depth R² curves use training data and do not establish out-of-sample predictive performance.
  • Some metrics stop contributing when fitted trees stop growing. The changing set of metrics can affect the mean curve and its candidate inflection points.
  • Inflections in model-fit curves are not independently validated ecological breakpoints.
  • The revised Random Forest classifier is not an exact reproduction of the original rounded-regression workflow. Original research inputs are needed to reproduce historical results.
  • The paper reflects the full author team; its overall performance measures are not presented here as scores from my separate exploratory fish/HIT analysis.

Learning & progression

How this work develops my practice.

This project shows a progression from retrieving spatial data through an external API to building and evaluating statistical workflows around it. The documented change from a rounded regressor to a classifier also makes a useful engineering distinction explicit: improving a reusable implementation can change its results, so historical outputs and revised code need separate provenance.
